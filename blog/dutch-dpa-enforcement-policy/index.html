A Regulator Has Opened a Case. What the Dutch DPA’s New Enforcement Policy Means in Practice

A practical guide for organisations that may hear from a data protection authority. This article is for general information only and is not legal advice.

On 30 October 2026, the Dutch data protection authority, the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens (AP), published a new enforcement policy in the official gazette, the Staatscourant, number 38702. The AP’s news item of the same day says the policy is meant to give organisations more clarity about what happens once an enforcement procedure starts. A draft went out for public consultation on 13 April 2026; the AP received 12 responses.

This is not a new fining scale, and it is not a piece about the cap on GDPR fines for small businesses. It is a map of the process: who gets copied, how to stop an ongoing infringement, and what you give up if you want a shorter route and a lower fine. The policy is written for the Netherlands, but the same questions arise at first contact with any regulator.

What the policy is, and how wide it runs

The official title is Handhavingsbeleid van de Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens.

Annex I lists the laws the AP can already enforce, including the GDPR, the Data Act, the Digital Services Act and the regulation on political advertising. The annex itself says the list will be supplemented, for example with implementing legislation for the AI Act. A first letter from the AP may therefore sit under more than one regime.

The policy also describes a toolkit that ranges from a reprimand or a closing letter to orders, processing bans and administrative fines. Instruments can be combined or escalated if the first one does not end the infringement. This article does not walk through how a fine is calculated. The process is the point.

Ending the infringement is the rule; a fine is not automatic

Section 2.1 sets out a principled duty to enforce, the beginselplicht tot handhaving. If the AP finds an infringement, that infringement must in principle be brought to an end, and the AP will use enforcement tools if needed to get there — for example if the organisation does not stop of its own accord. The duty is to end the infringement, to limit its consequences and to prevent repetition. It “does not mean that an administrative fine must always be imposed.” The AP decides in each case whether a fine is warranted.

A footnote points to Dutch case law and to the Court of Justice judgment of 26 September 2024 in Land Hessen (ECLI:EU:C:2024:785). The Court held, the policy recalls, that a supervisory authority is not obliged to impose a corrective measure, such as a fine, whenever a GDPR infringement is established, still less when that would not be appropriate, necessary or proportionate. That is not a promise of leniency. Section 3.1.7 allows the AP to close a case with an eindbrief, a closing letter, where the lawful situation has been restored and no formal measure is needed. The same section says this is not the default. “As a rule, infringements are too serious to be dealt with by a closing letter,” and there is room for one only in special circumstances of the case. A closing letter is not a decision, and the infringement can still be weighed if a later case arises.

So the first job, if you receive a formal notice, is not to argue about the size of a fine that may or may not come. It is to understand what the AP says is still going on, and to stop it if it is still going on.

Involve the DPO at once — the AP will, if it has the name

If the case concerns the GDPR or the Dutch police or judicial data laws, and the organisation has notified a data protection officer to the AP, section 2.5 says the DPO receives, at the start of the procedure, a copy of the investigation report or the draft decision and of the intention to enforce. The DPO also receives copies of the six-monthly progress updates and of other interim correspondence that goes to the merits of the case. If there is a hearing on the organisation’s written views, the DPO may attend as an observer, and later receives the hearing report even if not present. The DPO also receives a copy of the final enforcement decision, or of the letter in which the AP declines to take a measure.

The AP will inform the DPO. It will not treat the DPO as the organisation’s formal representative, because that would sit badly with the DPO’s independent position. The policy also reminds the organisation that the AP’s copies do not remove its own duty to involve the DPO properly and in good time, including in the written views (zienswijze) on the report or the intended decision.

If you have a DPO, the first formal letter is not something to park in a legal inbox and share later. Bring the DPO in on day one, even if the AP has already sent a copy. If you have appointed counsel, section 2.4 says the AP will in principle deal with that representative rather than with the organisation directly. Progress updates, if the statutory decision period is missed, come at least every six months.

For the wider GDPR roles, GDPR Made Simple — also on GDPRGard — and Mastering the GDPR — Volume 2: GDPR in Practice are plain-language background reading. They are not a manual for an AP procedure, and they do not tell you whether to settle a Dutch case.

Offer a concrete plan to stop the infringement — that is not an admission

Section 2.3 is the passage to use in the first internal meeting. During the enforcement process, and later during an objection or appeal, the AP is open to discussions with an organisation that wants to end the infringement, ahead of a formal decision. If you are not sure whether a planned change will actually end what the AP has found, you can talk to the case officers about a concrete proposal.

Two safeguards matter. First, making a proposal to end the infringement “does not count as an acknowledgement of the infringement.” Second, the proposal must be a clear and specific plan. If the organisation has appointed a DPO, the DPO’s advice on the proposal must be attached. The case officers will not advise you; they will test whether the plan would end the infringement that has been found. The policy recommends starting early, for example in parallel with the written views, so that the discussion is not cut short by a decision already taken.

Ending the infringement does not automatically take a fine off the table. The actual effects of stopping it can, however, be taken into account when the AP chooses an instrument, under the factors in section 4.2, including whether you stopped of your own accord and whether you did so before or after you knew about the investigation. Those factors are a reason to act quickly and to document what you did, not a scoring sheet for a fine.

The formal procedure usually begins when the AP sends an intention to enforce, after an investigation report or a proposed decision. Section 5.2 says that, where a formal instrument is intended, the procedure lasts on average nine to eighteen months. You may submit written views; an oral explanation can be added on request. The file then ends with a closing letter or with a decision to use one of the instruments.

The acknowledgement route: up to 35% off, at a real price

If the AP intends to impose an administrative fine, section 5.6 creates an acknowledgement and acquiescence procedure (erkennings- en berustingsprocedure). The AP may offer it, or the organisation may say it is interested, through the case officer. There is expressly no right to this route. The AP will use it only if the case lends itself to it and if sufficient efficiency gains are to be expected. It is meant to produce a shorter procedure and a final settlement, so that the organisation “clears the slate” and the AP keeps capacity for other cases.

The bargain is set out in three conditions:

  1. You acknowledge the infringement or infringements found in the report, and accept clearly and unambiguously that you are the offender and that the infringement is attributable to you.
  2. You agree to the applicable fining method and to the resulting fine, and you will not lodge an objection, an appeal or a further appeal.
  3. You agree to publication of the fine decision on the AP’s website. A accompanying notice is sent to the press, placed on the website and circulated on the AP’s social media. You may see the press text before publication, “solely to check for factual inaccuracies.”

In exchange, the AP reduces the fine by a maximum of 35%, applied to the amount determined under the policy’s fining section. This article does not calculate that amount.

Exploratory talks are confidential. New information you put forward is not used in the main case if no agreement is reached, unless you consent, for example because the material helps you. If talks succeed, the parties sign an agreement that governs how the fine decision is made. Either side may stop the procedure; the ordinary process then resumes. The route is available only once and is not restarted.

Whether that bargain is worth it depends on the case. If the facts or the legal assessment are genuinely in dispute, giving up the right to challenge, and accepting a public decision and a press release, is a high price. If the infringement is clear and what you want is finality, the discount may be worth weighing with counsel. The policy does not require you to take it.

Practical checklist: the first formal letter

  • ☐ Read the letter as the start of a procedure, not as a fine already imposed. A fine amount may not yet be stated.
  • ☐ Involve the DPO immediately, and confirm whether the AP already has the DPO on file. Brief the DPO yourself as well.
  • ☐ Instruct counsel if you need a formal representative. The AP will then, in principle, correspond with that person.
  • ☐ If the infringement is ongoing, stopping it is the first substantive task.
  • ☐ Prepare a clear, specific remediation plan, with a timeline and, where a DPO is appointed, the DPO’s advice attached. Offering that plan is not, under the policy, an admission.
  • ☐ Start that conversation early, in parallel with the written views.
  • ☐ Do not assume a closing letter. The policy treats that outcome as exceptional.
  • ☐ If a fine is in view, decide deliberately whether to ask about the acknowledgement procedure. There is no right to it. The price is admission, no appeal, and publication, in return for a reduction of up to 35%.
  • ☐ Treat exploratory settlement talks as confidential, and take advice before you share new facts.
  • ☐ This is a Dutch policy, useful as a map of one European regulator, not as a Europe-wide rulebook.

Conclusion

The AP has now written down how it wants an enforcement case to run. The duty that comes first is to end an ongoing infringement. A fine may follow, but it is not automatic, and a closing letter is not something to count on. If you have notified a DPO, that person is copied from the start, without becoming your representative. You can put a concrete stop-the-harm plan on the table without, in the AP’s own words, admitting the infringement. And if a fine is coming, there is a shorter path that can cut the amount by up to 35%, provided you are prepared to acknowledge the facts, waive a challenge and accept a public decision. The useful moment to read this policy is the day the first formal letter arrives.
