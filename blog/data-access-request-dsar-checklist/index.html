Got a Data Access Request? Your 30-Day Checklist

Based on Articles 12 and 15 of the GDPR and the EDPB's guidance, consulted on 24 October 2026. General information, not legal advice.

ZMBy Zirlandia Milkovic · 2026-10-31 · 4 min read

TL;DR

  • The legal deadline is one month from receipt, not exactly 30 days. Count it carefully.
  • You can extend by two further months for complex requests, but you must tell the person within the first month.
  • The first copy is normally free.
  • Only ask for ID if you have reasonable doubts about who's asking.
  • Can't or won't comply? Explain why within one month and mention their right to complain.

A data subject access request (DSAR) is someone asking: "What personal data do you have about me?" It might arrive by email, contact form or even a social media message. There's no required wording.

The title says "30 days" because that's how most people think of it. The GDPR actually says one month, so don't rely on a fixed day count.

Day 1: log it and start the clock

  • Record the date you received it. Article 12 says you must act "without undue delay and in any event within one month of receipt of the request."
  • Note who asked, how they asked and what they asked for.
  • Acknowledge receipt so the person knows you're on it.

Days 1–5: confirm who's asking

The EDPB's SME guide says you may ask for more information if you have "reasonable doubts" about the person's identity. One example: a request from an email address the customer doesn't usually use.

Don't demand ID by default. Ask only for what you need to be sure.

Days 3–15: find the data

Search everywhere the person's data could be:

  • email and shared inboxes,
  • customer database or CRM,
  • invoicing and accounting tools,
  • spreadsheets and shared drives,
  • newsletter and marketing tools,
  • AI tools or other services you've pasted customer data into.

If you keep a record of your processing, this step is much faster.

Days 10–20: prepare the response

Under Article 15, the person is entitled to a copy of their personal data and to information including:

  • the purposes of the processing,
  • the categories of data,
  • who it's been or will be shared with,
  • how long you'll keep it, or how you decide,
  • their rights, including to complain to a data protection authority,
  • where you got the data, if not from them,
  • whether automated decision-making is involved.

The right to a copy "shall not adversely affect the rights and freedoms of others." So remove other people's personal data where needed.

Day 20: decide on fees and extensions

  • Fees: the first copy is free. Article 15 allows "a reasonable fee based on administrative costs" for further copies. For a manifestly unfounded or excessive request, you may charge a reasonable fee or refuse, but you must be able to show it was unfounded or excessive.
  • Extension: for complex or multiple requests you can extend by two further months. Tell the person, with reasons, within the first month.

Before the month ends: send it

  • If the request came electronically, provide the information in a commonly used electronic form, unless they ask otherwise.
  • Send it securely.
  • If you won't comply, explain why within one month, and tell them they can complain to a supervisory authority and seek a judicial remedy.

After: keep a short log

Record what you received, what you sent and when. If anyone questions it later, that log is your evidence.
