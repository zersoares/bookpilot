Is Your Next Remote Hire Real? Deepfake Candidates and the GDPR Rules for Checking Them

Sources consulted on 3 November 2026.

ZMBy Zirlandia Milkovic · 2026-11-06 · 6 min read

TL;DR

  • The FBI says fraudulent remote IT workers have used AI and face-swapping technology in video job interviews to hide their identities.
  • Its advice includes identity checks during interviewing, onboarding and employment, and doing as much as possible in person.
  • In the EU, the GDPR still applies: collect only what's necessary, and be careful with anything that counts as biometric data.
  • The safest checks are low-tech: live video, document checks by a person, and verified references.

Remote hiring opened up talent everywhere. It also opened a door for people who aren't who they say they are.

What the FBI is seeing

In a January 2025 public service announcement, the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) warned that North Korean IT workers "have been observed using artificial intelligence and face-swapping technology during video job interviews to obfuscate their true identities". Once hired, some stole data and demanded ransoms.

In October 2026, IC3 added that these schemes increasingly rely on paid helpers who appear on camera, attend interviews or keep company laptops connected from their homes.

The FBI's warnings focus on US companies, but remote work has no borders, and the warning signs are useful anywhere.

Warning signs to watch for

From the FBI's guidance:

  • reluctance or inability to appear on camera, or inconsistencies when they do;
  • online profiles that don't match the CV, or several profiles with different photos;
  • the same phone number (often a VoIP number) or email reused across different applicants;
  • a delivery address for the laptop that is a freight forwarder or changes right after hiring;
  • requests to change payment platforms or accounts during onboarding.

The FBI also suggests a simple video test: ask the person to wave a hand in front of their face, which "may prompt a malfunction in AI generated video".

Where the GDPR comes in

Checking who you're hiring is legitimate. But in the EU, the way you check has to respect the GDPR. Three rules matter most.

1. Data minimisation

Article 5(1)(c) says personal data must be "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary". Ask: what do I actually need to confirm this person is real and is who they claim to be? Usually a document check and a live conversation, not a full copy of every ID document kept forever.

2. Biometric data is a special category

The GDPR defines biometric data as data from "specific technical processing" of physical characteristics that allows or confirms unique identification, such as facial images. Article 9 prohibits processing biometric data to uniquely identify someone unless an exception applies.

Recital 51 makes the distinction clear: photographs are covered by the definition of biometric data only "when processed through a specific technical means allowing the unique identification or authentication" of a person.

In practice: a recruiter looking at a candidate on video and comparing them with an ID photo is very different from running automated face matching. If you're considering a biometric identity-verification tool, stop and get advice first. You will likely need a clear Article 9 exception and a data protection impact assessment (Article 35).

3. Data protection by design

Article 25 requires measures that ensure, by default, that "only personal data which are necessary for each specific purpose" are processed. Build that into the hiring process from the start.

A proportionate verification process

  1. Tell candidates up front. Explain in your recruitment privacy notice what you check, why, and how long you keep it.
  2. Run live video interviews, camera on, with a person, not a recorded-only screen.
  3. Check ID by sight. Have the candidate hold the document up to the camera, as the FBI suggests. Record that the check was done rather than storing a copy, unless you have a legal reason to keep one.
  4. Verify references independently. Use contact details you find yourself, not ones the candidate supplies.
  5. Meet in person where you can, at least once before or soon after the start date.
  6. Control the equipment. Ship laptops only to a verified address, and give new hires only the access they need at first.
  7. Delete what you no longer need, including data on unsuccessful candidates, according to your retention policy.
