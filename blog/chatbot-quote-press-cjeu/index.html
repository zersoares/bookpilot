Can Your Chatbot Quote the Press? What the First CJEU Generative-AI Opinion Means for Live Retrieval

A practical guide for product teams, publishers and in-house counsel. This article is for general information only and is not legal advice.

On 3 November 2026, Advocate General Maciej Szpunar delivered his opinion in Case C-250/25, Like Company. It is the first generative-AI case to reach the Court of Justice of the European Union. The official press release is clear about where the risk sits. The questions about training large language models should, in his view, be declared inadmissible. The questions that matter concern what a chatbot does when it retrieves a press article in real time and turns it into an answer.

That distinction will surprise teams who have spent the past two years worrying only about the training set. If you run a chatbot that searches the live web, or “grounds” answers in current news, the legal pressure in this opinion is on the answer the user reads, not only on how the model was built.

The opinion is not a judgment. Advocates General propose a legal solution in complete independence; the Court is not bound by it. The judges have begun their deliberations, and judgment will come later. Until then, the official press release is the best public account of the reasoning. The full opinion is published on the CURIA website, but the Court’s case-file interface did not return the document text when checked for this article, so the facts below follow the Court’s own press release (No 155/26), not a paragraph-by-paragraph reading of the opinion.

What the case is about

Google Ireland, part of the Alphabet group, provides Google Gemini, a generative-AI chatbot that can converse in text or speech. Its large language models, the press release explains, use retrieval-augmented generation, also called RAG or “grounding”. The models collect information in real time from the web to make answers more accurate and reliable. That technology can be used to obtain information about the news. To provide those answers, the chatbot often retrieves, or “scrapes”, press articles available online.

Like Company, a Hungarian operator of various web portals, brought proceedings against Google before the Budapest Regional Court. It claims protection over articles published on those portals under the related right that EU law grants to press publishers over the “online use” of their “press publications”. That right is in Article 15(1) of Directive (EU) 2019/790, the Copyright in the Digital Single Market (CDSM) Directive. Like Company argues that Google used those articles to train the Gemini models and repeatedly reproduced them and made them available to the public through the chatbot between 13 June 2023 and 31 May 2024.

The Hungarian court stayed the case and referred four questions to the Court of Justice. Two ask whether using press publications to train an LLM falls under the publisher’s exclusive right of reproduction, or whether that use is freely permitted under the text and data mining exception in Article 4 of the same directive. The other two ask whether the answers provided to users may fall under the reproduction right, the right of making available to the public, or both.

Why the training questions are, in the Advocate General’s view, the wrong ones

Advocate General Szpunar advises the Court to declare the two training questions inadmissible. In his view, there is no evidence that Like Company’s articles were used to train the Gemini LLMs. He also takes the view that training an LLM is not a form of “online use” of press publications. Those questions are therefore, he says, hypothetical.

For completeness, he nonetheless suggests that the use of works and other subject matter protected under copyright and the related right to train an LLM is, as a rule, freely permitted under the text and data mining exception.

Two cautions follow. First, that suggestion is offered only in the alternative, after he has already advised that the training questions should not be answered. Second, it is still only an opinion. Teams that build or buy models should not treat it as a green light to ignore opt-outs, licences or technical access controls. The earlier fight over training data, and the separate market for licensing books and journals to AI tools, are not settled by this press release.

The test that actually matters: the answer the user reads

The Advocate General advises the Court to focus on the questions about the chatbot’s answers, because those are relevant in the main proceedings. When an LLM-driven chatbot retrieves a publication from a press publisher’s website, reuses that publication to generate an answer, and provides that answer to the user, the press publisher’s right may be infringed in certain circumstances.

He stresses that the publisher’s right does not protect the mere facts reported in press publications, only the specific form of expression. Its aim is to protect the investment that goes into making the publication, not to give publishers a monopoly over the news itself.

Whether such use falls under the publisher’s right of reproduction depends, in his view, on two factors:

  • First, whether the chatbot accessed the press publication unlawfully, in particular by circumventing effective technical measures — such as a paywall — designed to restrict access to, or web-scraping of, that publication.
  • Second, whether the answer merely reports facts from the original publication or, on the contrary, reuses the form of expression in a recognisable manner, to such an extent that the user, by reading the answer, is entirely dispensed from accessing the original publication.

The right of making available to the public is, as a rule, infringed where the form of expression of the press publication is systematically recognisable, to that same extent, in the answers provided to related user queries.

That is a practical test, even if the Court later adjusts it. A short factual recap, with a link to the source, is not the same as a rewrite that lets the reader skip the article. Circumventing a paywall or an anti-scraping measure is treated as a separate, serious problem. Facts are free; the wording is not.

When the chatbot provider is liable

The provider of the online chatbot is liable for such potential acts of use, the Advocate General says, where they are inherent to the business model of that provider, or where the provider has failed to put in place appropriate measures to counter those acts credibly and effectively.

The press release does not list those measures. It does not say, for example, how often a quotation may appear, how a citation should look, or what counts as a “credible and effective” filter. Those details, if they appear in the full opinion, were not independently verified for this article. What the official summary does say is enough to shape a design review: if live retrieval of press articles is part of how the product works, the provider cannot treat unauthorised reuse as a surprise caused only by the model.

A preliminary ruling also has a limited job. The Court of Justice will interpret EU law; it will not itself decide the Hungarian dispute. The Budapest Regional Court will apply the eventual judgment to the facts of Like Company’s claim. Other national courts will be bound in the usual way where the same point of law arises. That is a further reason not to treat today’s opinion as the last word, and not to ignore it either. It is the first detailed public signal from the Court’s judicial arm about generative AI, and it puts live retrieval at the centre of the analysis.

For product teams, a useful habit is to keep a sample of news answers and compare them with the source articles. If a reasonably careful reader can see the original wording, or can leave the source unopened because the answer has already done the work of the article, you are in the territory the Advocate General describes. A citation and a link help the reader, but they do not, by themselves, answer the “dispensed from accessing the original” test.

What this means if you build or buy a web-grounded chatbot

If your assistant answers questions about current events by fetching pages on the fly, map the path from query to answer. Which sources can it reach? Does it honour robots rules, logins, paywalls and other technical restrictions? Does the answer reproduce distinctive phrasing, structure or extended passages, or does it state facts and send the reader to the source? Can a user who reads only the answer skip the original article entirely? How often does that happen for news queries?

Those questions are a review method, not a compliance certificate. They follow the two-factor test in the press release. They also help you separate this copyright issue from a different chatbot risk: leaking personal data. If your bot also handles customer information, When Your AI Chatbot Leaks Data is an English-language guide to chatbot privacy and pre-launch tests with dummy data. It does not cover press publishers’ rights, and it is not a substitute for advice on this opinion.

This article is also not about licensing books to AI tools, or about scraping the open web to train a model. Those are related markets, but the Like Company opinion, as presented by the Court, is about live retrieval and the answer on the screen.

Practical checklist: live retrieval of press content

  • ☐ Treat the Advocate General’s opinion as guidance, not as the judgment. The Court can still take a different path.
  • ☐ Map every live-retrieval path in your product: query, retrieved URL, prompt, answer, citations and any cached copies.
  • ☐ Do not bypass effective access controls. Paywalls, login walls and anti-scraping measures are part of the test described in the press release.
  • ☐ Separate facts from expression. A factual recap is not the same as a recognisable reuse of the article’s wording.
  • ☐ Ask whether the answer dispenses the user from opening the original. If it does, so that the reader can skip the source, that is the scenario the opinion flags.
  • ☐ Look for a pattern, not only one reply. The making-available analysis, as summarised, concerns systematic recognisability in answers to related queries.
  • ☐ Document the measures you actually run, not only a policy sentence: source allowlists, refusal to fetch gated pages, quotation limits, mandatory citations and links, human review for news answers, and logging.
  • ☐ Do not assume the text-and-data-mining exception covers live answers. In this opinion, that exception is discussed in relation to training, and only in the alternative.
  • ☐ If you are a publisher, review how your articles are exposed to automated retrieval, and what technical measures you actually enforce.
  • ☐ Wait for the judgment before rebuilding your legal basis around this opinion alone.

Conclusion

The first generative-AI case at the Court of Justice is easy to misread as a training case. In the Advocate General’s account, the training questions should not even be answered. The live path is the one that matters: a chatbot that fetches a press article in real time and writes an answer the user may never need to leave.

Facts are not protected. Circumventing a paywall, and handing the reader a recognisable substitute for the article, may be. Providers are in the frame when that reuse is built into the business, or when they have not put credible measures in place to stop it. None of this is law until the Court rules. It is, however, a clear warning to anyone whose product quotes the news.
