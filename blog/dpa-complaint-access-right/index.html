You Complained to a Data Protection Authority. Do You Have a Right of Access to Your File?

A practical guide for complainants, small businesses and privacy teams. This article is for general information only and is not legal advice.

On 5 November 2026, the Third Chamber of the Court of Justice of the European Union delivered its judgment in Case C-205/25, Lindenberg. A journalist had complained to Bavaria’s data protection authority and then asked, under Article 15 of the GDPR, to see the file. Bavarian law said, as a general rule, that there was no right of access to a supervisory authority’s files or records. The Court disagreed with that blanket exclusion.

The judgment does two things that matter in practice. First, when a person lodges a complaint under Article 77 of the GDPR, the authority is also a “controller” of that person’s personal data, and must in principle grant access to those data and to the information listed in Article 15. Second, Article 23 cannot support a national rule that cuts off that right across the board.

It also draws a line that is just as important: Article 15 is not a right to the entire administrative file. The text used for this article is the judgment as published on IPCuria, marked as a provisional text. No official Court press release for this case was found, and the EUR-Lex page returned no document text when checked.

How the dispute reached Luxembourg

Joachim Lindenberg is a journalist and runs a blog that deals with data protection, among other topics. Since 2021 he has lodged several complaints with the Bayerisches Landesamt für Datenschutzaufsicht, the Bavarian data protection authority.

On 13 May 2022 he lodged a complaint, and the authority opened a supervisory procedure against a third party. On 11 October 2022, the authority emailed him to say it had found that the third party had breached data protection rules and that, after a deadline to remedy those breaches, a penalty notice would be issued if further breaches occurred. The same day, Mr Lindenberg asked for more details of the measures taken. When he received no response, he requested full details under Article 15(1) and (3) of the GDPR.

On 20 October 2022 the authority refused. It relied on Paragraph 20(2) of the Bavarian Data Protection Act (BayDSG), which, the judgment says, expressly precluded the right of access or inspection with respect to the files and records of the supervisory authorities.

Mr Lindenberg challenged that refusal before the Bavarian Administrative Court in Ansbach, asking to be given a copy of all the information in the file relating to the procedure opened after his complaint. On 23 February 2024 the authority granted him electronic access to the file, without acknowledging any legal obligation to do so. He still sought a declaration that the October 2022 refusal was unlawful.

The Ansbach court stayed the case and asked the Court of Justice two questions. Is a supervisory authority handling an Article 77 complaint also a “controller” under Article 4(7), and therefore required to grant Article 15 access? If so, does EU law, in particular Article 23, preclude a national rule that excludes, in a blanket manner, rights of access or inspection in respect of the authority’s files and records?

The authority is a controller of the complainant’s data

The Court answered the first question yes, in principle.

A supervisory authority that receives a complaint must itself process the complainant’s personal data: those data are recorded, consulted, stored and used. The purposes and means of that processing arise, in essence, from the EU rules that govern the authority’s work. It must therefore be regarded as a controller of the complainant’s personal data.

The Court underlined why that classification matters. If the authority were not a controller, the complainant would lose, as regards that processing, the Chapter III rights: transparent information, access, rectification, erasure and restriction of processing. The authority would also fall outside the Article 5 principles and the security rules in Chapter IV. The Bavarian authority’s argument that the GDPR keeps the roles of “supervisory authority” and “controller” strictly apart did not succeed. Those provisions, the Court said, describe the usual situation in which the two roles do not overlap; they do not prevent an authority from being a controller in some circumstances.

The consequence is specific. In a complaint procedure, the data subject must, in accordance with Article 15(1), be granted a right of access to personal data concerning him or her, and to the information listed in Article 15.

What Article 15 does not give you

Then comes the limit, stated in paragraph 46 of the judgment:

Article 15(1) “establishes only a right of the data subject to access the personal data concerning him or her and a right to information on the processing of those data. By contrast, that article does not establish either a general right of access to the administrative file or a right to inspect all the documents held by the public authority in its capacity as controller.”

That sentence should be read slowly if you have just complained, or if your organisation is the one being investigated. The complainant can ask the authority for their personal data and the usual Article 15 information: purposes, categories, recipients, retention, and so on. They cannot, on the basis of Article 15 alone, demand every note, email, draft and attachment in the case file. The company’s confidential material, and other people’s data, are not automatically on the table.

This is the opposite of a fishing expedition, and it is also the opposite of a wall. The authority has to give the complainant access to data that are about the complainant. It does not have to open the whole dossier.

A blanket national ban is not a valid restriction

The second question was whether Article 23 allows a general national exclusion of that access right. The Court held that Article 23(1)(c), (h) and (i) preclude a provision under which the complainant has no right of access to personal data concerning him or her that are held by a supervisory authority.

The GDPR allows restrictions on Article 15 by legislation, but only to safeguard listed objectives, and only if the restriction respects the essence of the rights and is necessary and proportionate. The explanatory memorandum to the BayDSG referred to Article 23 without naming any of those objectives. The authority argued that the rule protected the confidentiality of third parties’ data, the proper functioning and independence of supervisory authorities, and public security.

On third-party confidentiality, the Court recalled recital 63: access should not adversely affect the rights or freedoms of others, but the result should not be a refusal to provide all information. Where rights collide, a balance must be struck. Suitable measures, “such as redaction or anonymisation of personal data concerning third parties,” make it possible to reconcile the interests “without completely removing the right of access.” A rule that requires the controller to refuse all access, with no balancing exercise, is not compatible with Article 23(1)(i).

On the functioning and independence of authorities, the Court accepted that this is a legitimate concern, but held that the right of access cannot be precluded in a general manner for that purpose under Article 23(1)(h). On public security, Article 23(1)(c) can justify a restriction only where there is a genuine and sufficiently serious threat affecting a fundamental interest of society. No such threat was apparent from the documents before the Court.

In short: authorities may withhold or redact what must be withheld. They may not answer every access request with a general statutory “no”.

What this means if you complained, or if the complaint is about you

If you have an open complaint, you can ask the authority for the personal data it holds about you and for the Article 15 information. Expect names of third parties, and parts of the file that are not your data, to be redacted or withheld. A later, voluntary disclosure — as happened in this case in 2024 — does not, by itself, decide whether the earlier refusal was lawful. That remains for the national court.

If your organisation is the target of the complaint, this judgment is not a right for the complainant to see your entire correspondence with the authority. The file contains the complainant’s data, and the authority must handle access to those data. Your own rights, trade secrets and other people’s data still have to be weighed. Redaction is the tool the Court points to, not a total lock on the file.

This is also a different situation from a data subject access request sent to your company. When a customer or employee asks you for their data, the clock and the workflow are the ones described in BookPilot’s 30-day DSAR checklist and in The DSAR Response Kit. The same kit is part of GDPRGard’s Compliance Field Kit. Those resources help you answer a request made to the business. They do not tell a supervisory authority how to open its own file, and they do not create a right to inspect every document in a complaint procedure.

Practical checklist: access after a GDPR complaint

  • ☐ If you are the complainant, ask for your personal data and the Article 15 information, not “the entire file” as if Article 15 were a freedom-of-information right.
  • ☐ Be specific about the procedure and the data you mean, so the authority can find what relates to you.
  • ☐ Expect redaction or anonymisation of third-party data, not a complete refusal and not an unredacted dump.
  • ☐ If you are refused under a general national rule, the Lindenberg judgment is relevant: a blanket exclusion of access to the complainant’s own data is, in the Court’s view, precluded by Article 23.
  • ☐ If your organisation is under investigation, involve counsel and your DPO early. Decide what in the file is genuinely confidential, and be ready to explain why.
  • ☐ Do not assume the complainant will see your side of the correspondence. Article 15 does not grant a general right to inspect all documents.
  • ☐ Keep your own DSAR process separate. A request sent to your company still follows the ordinary Article 15 rules and deadlines.
  • ☐ Remember that this judgment interprets EU law. The national court still has to apply it to the Bavarian case, and other Member States’ file-access rules will need their own reading against the same articles.
  • ☐ Use the official judgment, not secondary summaries, when you rely on a particular paragraph. The IPCuria text used here is marked provisional.

Conclusion

A complaint to a data protection authority is not a black box in which the complainant’s own data disappear. In Lindenberg, the Court held that the authority is a controller of those data and must, in principle, grant Article 15 access. A national law that shuts that door in every case cannot be saved by a general appeal to independence, confidentiality or public security.

The same judgment closes a different door. Article 15 is not a key to the whole administrative file. The balance, where other people’s rights are involved, is redaction or anonymisation, not a total “no” and not a total “yes”. That is a workable rule for complainants and for the organisations they complain about, as long as no one confuses a right of access to your data with a right to read everyone’s file.
