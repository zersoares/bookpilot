Before You Give an AI Agent Access to Your Inbox: A 10-Minute Permissions Check

Sources consulted on 3 November 2026.

ZMBy Zirlandia Milkovic · 2026-11-04 · 5 min read

TL;DR

  • OWASP ranks Excessive Agency third in its 2026 Top 10 for LLM applications, calling it "the most consequential move on the list".
  • Its root causes are too much functionality, too many permissions and too much autonomy.
  • Before you click Allow, check what the agent can read, what it can change and what it can do without asking you.
  • Then review the apps you've already connected. Old access doesn't expire just because you forgot about it.

AI agents are useful because they can act: sort your inbox, draft replies, book meetings, file documents. To do that, they ask for access, usually through a screen that says something like "This app wants to read, compose and send email on your behalf."

Most of us click Allow without reading. With an agent, that habit is worth breaking.

Why permissions matter more with agents

The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2026 describes Excessive Agency as the vulnerability that "enables damaging actions to be performed in response to unexpected, ambiguous or manipulated outputs from an LLM". It lists the usual triggers: a model that misunderstands a vague instruction, or a prompt injection hidden in content the agent reads, such as an email.

OWASP says the root cause is typically one or more of three things:

  • Excessive functionality: the agent has tools it doesn't need.
  • Excessive permissions: it can reach more data than the task requires.
  • Excessive autonomy: it can take high-impact actions without a human checking.

In 2026 OWASP moved Excessive Agency up to third place, noting that "agentic deployments are where the damage is landing".

You don't need to be a developer to apply this. You just need to ask three questions before you connect anything.

The 10-minute check

1. What can it read? (3 minutes)

Look at the consent screen. Does a meeting scheduler really need to read every email you've ever received, or only your calendar? If the app offers a narrower option, take it. If it doesn't, ask whether this tool is the right one.

2. What can it change or send? (3 minutes)

Read access and write access are very different risks. "Read your files" is one thing; "edit and delete your files" or "send email as you" is another. OWASP's own example: an agent that only needs to read documents shouldn't get a tool that can also modify and delete them.

3. What can it do without asking? (2 minutes)

OWASP recommends requiring human approval for high-impact actions. Look in the agent's settings for an approval step before it sends messages, deletes anything, shares files or makes payments. Turn it on.

4. Write it down (2 minutes)

Keep a simple list: tool, account connected, access granted, date, and why. It takes two minutes and makes the next review easy.

Clean up what you already connected

Every app you've signed into with your Google or Microsoft account may still hold access.

  • Google: open your Google Account and find the section listing third-party apps and services connected to it. Google's help page explains how to review each app and remove its access. Removing access stops future access; it doesn't delete data the app already stored.
  • Microsoft 365 (admins): in Microsoft Entra, open Enterprise apps, select an app and choose Permissions to see what was granted by an admin and by individual users. Microsoft also documents how to restrict whether users can consent to apps themselves.

Remove anything you don't recognise or no longer use.

A simple rule for your team

Agree on one sentence and share it: "No AI agent gets write or send access to company email, files or payments without a second person approving it."
