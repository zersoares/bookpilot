The First AI Act Harmonised Standard Is Out: What EN 18286 Means Before the High-Risk Rules Apply

A practical guide for teams that build, integrate or buy AI that may be high-risk. This article is for general information only and is not legal advice.

The European Commission has published, with a publication date of 19 October 2026, the reference to the first harmonised standard supporting the implementation of the AI Act in the Official Journal of the European Union. The standard is EN 18286:2026, Artificial intelligence — Quality management system for EU AI Act regulatory purposes, developed by CEN and CENELEC.

According to the Commission, publication is made through Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/2274. Providers that implement the standard will benefit from a presumption of conformity with the Article 17 quality-management requirements covered by the standard. The Official Journal pages for that decision returned no document text when checked on 10 November 2026 (HTTP 202), so this article does not cite a journal issue number or a precise OJ date beyond what the Commission’s own pages state.

The timing is the practical point. High-risk obligations are not all due tomorrow. The Commission says the standard arrives ahead of the application of requirements for high-risk AI systems. That gives providers — and the smaller firms that build or integrate tools in areas such as recruitment — a window to put a quality-management system in place while the presumption of conformity is already on the table.

What a harmonised standard actually does

A harmonised standard is a technical document prepared by the European standardisation organisations on the basis of a Commission request. Once the Commission is satisfied that the standard meets the legal requirements, it publishes a reference in the Official Journal. Applying the standard remains voluntary. Providers can use another framework to show that they meet the AI Act. A standard that has been referenced in the Official Journal, however, gives legal certainty: companies that apply it are presumed to comply with the legal requirements the standard covers.

The Commission’s standardisation page spells that mechanism out, and it places EN 18286 in a longer programme. CEN and CENELEC, working through Joint Technical Committee 21, have been asked to develop standards in ten areas, including risk management, data quality, record keeping, transparency, human oversight, accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity, quality management and conformity assessment. EN 18286 is the first of those to be referenced in the Official Journal. Further harmonised standards are expected to follow. A presumption of conformity with Article 17 is not a presumption of conformity with the rest of the high-risk chapter.

What EN 18286 is designed to do

The Commission’s library page is the source for the following description of the standard itself.

EN 18286 translates the Article 17 requirements into an operational quality-management system (QMS). The Commission calls it the only AI QMS standard with a presumption of conformity. It is mapped to Article 17, built around the AI system lifecycle rather than around the organisation, and designed to be layered on top of existing international standards — such as ISO/IEC 42001, ISO 9001, or a sectoral QMS such as ISO 13485 — rather than to replace them.

It explicitly allows providers in regulated sectors to integrate management systems, rather than run separate ones. Organisations that do not already have ISO/IEC 42001, and that plan to place or put into service high-risk AI systems on the EU market, can use EN 18286 to expand an existing QMS or to build one that fulfils the Article 17 requirements.

Two design choices are worth lingering on. First, the unit of management is the AI system’s life, from design and data through testing, release and monitoring, not a generic corporate quality manual that never mentions the model. Second, you are not being asked to throw away a QMS you already run for medical devices, information security or general quality. The standard is meant to sit on top of that work.

This article does not summarise the clauses of EN 18286 itself. The official standard is a CEN/CENELEC document, and the Implementing Decision was not opened on EUR-Lex. The Commission’s description is what can be verified here.

What Article 17 actually requires

Article 17 of the AI Act requires providers of high-risk AI systems to put a quality-management system in place that ensures compliance with the regulation. The system must be documented in a systematic and orderly way, as written policies, procedures and instructions. The official text, as presented by the Commission’s AI Act Service Desk, lists at least the following aspects: a strategy for regulatory compliance, including conformity assessment and the management of modifications; design control and verification; development, quality control and quality assurance; examination, test and validation procedures, and how often they are carried out; technical specifications, including standards, and what will be used if a harmonised standard is not applied in full; data management throughout the life of the system; the risk-management system in Article 9; post-market monitoring; serious-incident reporting; communication with authorities and other parties; record-keeping; resource management; and an accountability framework for management and staff.

The implementation of those aspects must be proportionate to the size of the provider’s organisation, in particular if the provider is an SME, including a start-up, or an SMC. Providers must still respect the degree of rigour and the level of protection required to ensure compliance. Providers that already have quality-management obligations under sectoral Union law may include the Article 17 aspects in that existing system. For certain financial institutions, parts of the obligation are deemed fulfilled by complying with internal-governance rules under Union financial-services law, taking harmonised standards into account.

In plain terms: if you are a provider of a high-risk system, you need a documented QMS that covers the system’s life, and it can be sized to your organisation, but it cannot be a thin policy that never touches design, data, testing or monitoring.

When the high-risk rules actually apply

Most small businesses use AI; they do not place high-risk systems on the market. They are deployers, not providers, and the everyday chatbot or writing tool usually sits outside the high-risk category. The BookPilot article EU AI Act for Small Businesses: What Actually Applies to You walks through that distinction. This standard is aimed at providers of high-risk systems. It still matters if you build, fine-tune or integrate a tool that may fall into a high-risk use, for example in recruitment, credit or education.

Following the Digital Omnibus on AI, the Commission has published a revised enforcement timeline. On its page of guidelines for high-risk systems, and on the AI Act Service Desk timeline, the dates are:

  • 2 December 2027: rules for high-risk AI systems in Annex III apply. The Commission’s guidelines page lists, among the areas concerned, biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, migration, asylum and border control.
  • 2 August 2028: rules for high-risk AI embedded in regulated products covered by Annex I apply. The guidelines page gives robotics and industrial machinery as examples.

Those dates are the reason a QMS standard published in October 2026 is useful now rather than in late 2027. A quality-management system that follows the system’s lifecycle takes time to design, to layer onto ISO 9001 or ISO/IEC 42001, to test and to document. Waiting until the obligation applies is a way to miss the presumption that already exists.

For a wider calendar of what already applies — prohibitions, AI literacy, general-purpose model rules, Article 50 transparency — see GDPRGard’s 2026 to 2028 compliance calendar and EU AI Act, Without the Scare Tactics. If you are mainly a deployer of everyday tools, GDPRGard’s AI Tools Compliance Pack is a practical starting point for use at work; it is not a substitute for an Article 17 QMS, and it does not make a system high-risk or not.

What to start doing in the window you now have

First, decide whether you are a provider of a system that may be high-risk, a deployer of someone else’s system, or both. If you only use a vendor’s recruitment tool, your job is to ask how that vendor will meet Article 17 and the rest of the high-risk rules, not to write a QMS for a product you do not place on the market. If you build or substantially modify the system, the QMS question is yours.

Second, inventory what quality system you already have. ISO 9001, ISO 13485, ISO/IEC 42001 or a sectoral framework can be a base. The Commission’s description of EN 18286 is that it is meant to be layered on, not to replace those standards.

Third, map Article 17’s list onto the lifecycle of the actual system: how you design and change it, how you get and manage data, how you test, how you monitor it after release, how you report serious incidents, and who is accountable. A quality manual that never names the model is not what Article 17 describes.

Fourth, remember that the presumption covers the Article 17 requirements that the standard covers, not every high-risk duty. Risk management, data governance, logging, transparency, human oversight and cybersecurity have their own forthcoming standards in the Commission’s request.

Practical checklist: using EN 18286 before 2027

  • ☐ Classify your role. Are you a provider of a potentially high-risk system, a deployer, or both? Most everyday business use is not high-risk.
  • ☐ Check the Annex III and Annex I dates: 2 December 2027 for stand-alone high-risk use cases such as employment and education; 2 August 2028 for AI embedded in regulated products, according to the Commission and the AI Act Service Desk.
  • ☐ Treat EN 18286 as voluntary, but as the path that carries a presumption of conformity with the Article 17 requirements it covers.
  • ☐ Do not read that presumption as covering the whole high-risk chapter. Other standards are still being developed.
  • ☐ If you already run ISO 9001, ISO/IEC 42001 or a sectoral QMS, plan to layer EN 18286 on top, rather than to start from a blank page, which is how the Commission describes the standard.
  • ☐ Build the QMS around the system’s lifecycle, not only around the organisation chart.
  • ☐ Document the Article 17 topics in writing: design control, testing, data management, risk management, post-market monitoring, incident reporting, record-keeping and accountability.
  • ☐ Keep the system proportionate to your size, as Article 17 requires for SMEs, without dropping the level of protection the Act requires.
  • ☐ If you buy a potentially high-risk tool, ask the vendor how its QMS will meet Article 17, and whether it intends to apply EN 18286.
  • ☐ The Implementing Decision itself was not opened on EUR-Lex for this article. Rely on the Commission’s published description until you can read the Official Journal text and the standard.

Conclusion

The first harmonised standard under the AI Act is a quality-management standard, not a full rulebook for high-risk AI. That is still a meaningful step. Article 17 is the backbone of how a provider is supposed to run a high-risk system, and EN 18286 is now the official route to a presumption of conformity with the parts of that article the standard covers.

The high-risk rules for Annex III systems apply from 2 December 2027, and for many product-embedded systems from 2 August 2028. The standard is already here. Organisations that may be providers — including small teams building hiring or credit tools — can start from the quality system they have, map it to the system’s lifecycle, and use the window, rather than waiting for a deadline that will not come with a ready-made QMS attached.
