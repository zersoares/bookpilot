Smart Glasses at Work and in the Shop: What the EDPB Expects From Manufacturers, Providers and Employers

A practical guide for workplaces, shops and anyone who sells or integrates smart glasses. This article is for general information only and is not legal advice.

On 13 October 2026, at its 124th plenary meeting, the European Data Protection Board adopted Statement 1/2026 on smart glasses. The same day it published an external report, The connected gaze, prepared in the Support Pool of Experts programme at the request of the French data protection authority. The report was written by an external contractor, and the EDPB’s news item states that its views do not necessarily reflect the Board’s official position. This article therefore follows the statement itself, not the report.

The statement is preliminary guidance, not a new regulation. It tries to answer the questions that arise as soon as connected glasses appear in an office, a shop or a customer-facing role: when does the GDPR apply, who is the controller, and how can people nearby tell that they are being recorded?

What the Board means by smart glasses

Smart glasses are wearable devices that often look like regular glasses or sunglasses, and that incorporate cameras, microphones and software or applications, including AI systems, for purposes such as translation or object identification. They may connect to the internet or to a phone, and may be operated by voice or by other inputs.

Where the glasses record or otherwise process information relating to an identified or identifiable person, they process personal data and the GDPR applies, except in the cases listed in Article 2(2), including purely personal or household activities.

Common uses include calls, photographs and video, real-time translation, live streaming and “augmented” overlays, in personal or professional settings. The concern, the statement says, is that unlike many other recording devices, smart glasses can allow a near-invisible collection of personal data, because they are hard to distinguish from ordinary eyewear. Some devices may record background conversations in which the wearer is not taking part. Use together with facial or voice recognition “raises unprecedented questions” and poses a high risk to rights and freedoms in public spaces. Even if the device can be identified as smart glasses, people may still object to being filmed, or may change how they behave if they think they are under unvetted surveillance. Users, the Board says, should respect others’ privacy and dignity, and should explicitly inform other people that the glasses are in use.

Certain places are flagged as especially sensitive, including medical facilities, changing rooms, swimming pools and other private or intimate situations. Use in the workplace may raise concerns about employee monitoring and about the confidentiality of the organisation’s information, including trade secrets. Recording or audio capture may be inappropriate there, or prohibited under national law or local rules. Some establishments, a footnote records, have already restricted smart glasses on their premises. The workplace and the shop floor are the focus of this article.

When the wearer is a controller — and when the household exemption may apply

Responsibility can fall on different actors: the user, the producer of the device, or the provider of a linked application or service, including an AI system. One of them may be a sole controller; two or more may be joint controllers. The test is who determines the purposes and means of the processing.

The user’s position depends on how the glasses are used. The user is not subject to the GDPR if the actions are a purely personal or household activity, outside any professional or commercial context — for example sharing a photo or video only with a family member or a friend, or using the device for personal translation in a private context. That exemption, the Board says, must be narrowly construed and assessed case by case. It would not apply if the photo or video is shared with an indefinite number of people on a public social media account, or if the glasses are used in official, professional, political or other non-personal activities. In those situations the user would be a controller or joint controller, and would have to comply with the GDPR, notably the transparency rules.

Even when the GDPR does not apply to the user, other law still might, including national criminal, civil or labour law. Failure to comply with those rules may also, depending on the circumstances, call into question the fairness of the processing under Article 5(1)(a) of the GDPR. For an employer or a shop, the practical line is reasonably clear. Glasses used as a work tool, in official business, or to create public content are not a household hobby. The organisation, the wearer, or both, may be controllers.

Manufacturers and service providers cannot hand their duties to the user

The statement is just as clear on the other side of the relationship. The GDPR still applies to controllers or processors that provide the means for processing personal data, even for personal or household activities — including the producer of the glasses and the provider of a linked application or service. Those actors may be controllers where they determine the purposes and means of the processing, for example an AI feature that analyses a camera image in which other people appear.

Where those actors are controllers, they are responsible for their own processing. They “should not rely on the users’ actions to meet their own GDPR obligations, such as transparency toward other individuals whose data might be processed when the smart glasses are being used.” If they further process the data for a new purpose, that purpose must remain compatible with the original one.

The EDPB Chair, Anu Talus, put the same point in the Board’s news item: the producer and the provider of a linked service cannot transfer their obligations to users.

Further processing for commercial purposes beyond the service the user asked for, or for AI training, is singled out as especially hard to justify on legitimate interest, because bystanders would not expect it, particularly if they were not aware of the initial collection. Those are warning lights for product design, not a complete ban stated in the statement. If you already worry about what connected apps collect in the background, The Hidden Risk of Connected Apps is a BookPilot guide to that wider problem. It is not a smart-glasses manual, and it does not replace the statement.

Transparency: recording has to be obvious

Compliance with the GDPR’s transparency rules is, the Board says, a major challenge, because of how discreet the devices are. Bystanders may not know that data about them are being processed, by whom, or for what purpose. Some functions, such as translation or AI features, may be particularly invisible. Traditional privacy notices, written for users, “do not seem sufficient or appropriate” to inform non-users, especially when the controller is a producer or service provider with no relationship with the people recorded.

The statement therefore asks controllers and producers to combine a range of mechanisms, including, by default, in-the-moment signals that processing is taking place. Those measures, a footnote says, should be impossible or difficult to disable, and easy for bystanders to notice. The examples include visual or LED indicators, visible in many lighting conditions and designed so that information cannot be recorded when the indicator is covered; audio indicators and voice-activation cues, such as a shutter sound; smartphone proximity notifications, which should not be the sole method, should be possible to refuse, and should not enable tracking of the wearer; and a design that distinguishes the glasses from ordinary eyewear.

Whenever transparency depends on technical measures, those measures must be effective, hard to circumvent, and updated when necessary. The Board also recommends industry-wide standards and awareness campaigns. Chair Talus summarised the same expectation in the news item: recording should be obvious — for instance with bright LED lights, audio signals, voice-activation cues, privacy-friendly smartphone notifications, or unique designs — and the warnings should be reliable, hard to ignore, and kept up to date.

A short policy for the workplace and the shop

For employers, the workplace remark is the place to start. Smart glasses used as part of the job may amount to employee monitoring, and may expose confidential business information. Before anyone is asked, or allowed, to wear them at work, decide whether the use is necessary, what is recorded, where recording is banned, how long footage is kept, and who can see it. Staff and visitors need a way to know that recording is happening that does not depend on an intranet policy. GDPRGard’s HR Compliance Pack and BookPilot’s GDPR for HR Departments cover employee and candidate data more generally; they are not written as smart-glasses policies, but they are a starting point for the HR side of the same problem.

For a shop or a customer-facing service, the same logic applies to people who walk in. If staff wear glasses that can record, the recording has to be obvious, and you should assume that the organisation, not only the employee, may be a controller. You may also need a house rule that visitors cannot use recording glasses on the premises. If footage goes to the cloud, the security questions are the ordinary ones in GDPRGard’s GDPR Cybersecurity Guide. That guide does not make a device lawful on its own.

The statement also lists technical mitigations: limiting continuous recording, live streaming and retention; privacy-friendly defaults, including blurring bystanders, with on-device processing where possible; disabling persistent storage by default where the product is meant only to give real-time assistance; location-based limits in sensitive places; and “privacy frictions” that slow down sharing to social media. A user, the Board says, should not justify continuous or indiscriminate recording of other people solely by a general or hypothetical concern for personal safety.

Practical checklist: smart glasses at work and in public-facing premises

  • ☐ Treat Statement 1/2026 as preliminary guidance, not as a new statute. The GDPR and national labour, civil and criminal law still apply.
  • ☐ Decide, in writing, when glasses may be used as a work or shop tool, and where recording is prohibited.
  • ☐ Identify the controller for each use: the employer or shop, the wearer, the manufacturer, the app or AI provider, or more than one of them.
  • ☐ Do not rely on the household exemption for professional, official or public social-media use. The Board says that exemption is narrow.
  • ☐ If you make or power the device, do not shift your transparency duties onto the wearer.
  • ☐ Make recording obvious by default: a visible indicator, an audio cue, a distinctive design, and any phone notification as a complement, not as the only warning. Those signals should be hard to disable or cover, and kept up to date.
  • ☐ Tell staff and visitors what is recorded, why, and how long it is kept, at the moment of recording, not only in a privacy policy.
  • ☐ Minimise what is stored. If the product is for real-time assistance, persistent storage may not be necessary and, the Board says, should be off by default.
  • ☐ Review vendors and connected apps for further use of footage, including training or commercial profiling that bystanders would not expect.

Conclusion

Smart glasses look like ordinary eyewear, and that is precisely the data-protection problem. The EDPB’s first statement does not invent new law. It applies the old questions — who is the controller, is this really a household use, can people nearby tell what is happening — to a device that can record a room without looking like a camera.

For manufacturers and app providers, the duties stay with them; they cannot be handed to the wearer. For employers and shops, professional use is not a private pastime, and a recording that cannot be noticed is hard to defend. The minimum the Board describes is harder to design than to state: when the glasses are recording, it should be obvious. A short workplace and shop policy, written before the first pair is issued, is the way to take that seriously.
