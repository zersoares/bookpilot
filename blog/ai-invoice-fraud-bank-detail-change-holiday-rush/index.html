"We've Changed Our Bank Details": How to Stop AI-Powered Invoice Fraud Before the Holiday Rush

Sources consulted on 3 November 2026.

ZMBy Zirlandia Milkovic · 2026-11-05 · 5 min read

  • That made it the second-costliest crime type reported to IC3, after investment fraud.

  • The fix isn't technical: verify every bank-detail change by phone, using a number you already had.

November and December are busy. Suppliers send final invoices, budgets close, people go on holiday and everyone wants to pay before the year ends. That pressure is exactly what payment fraudsters count on.

How the scam works

Europol describes invoice fraud simply. Someone pretends to represent a supplier you already pay. They contact you by email, phone or letter and ask you to change the bank details for future invoices. The new account belongs to the fraudster.

Sometimes the email comes from a lookalike address, with one character different. Sometimes it comes from the supplier's real mailbox, because that mailbox was hacked. The invoice looks right, the amount is right and the tone is right.

What the numbers say

The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) 2025 report shows:

  • 24,768 business email compromise (BEC) complaints;
  • $3,046,598,558 in reported BEC losses;
  • BEC second only to investment fraud by reported loss.

These figures come from complaints voluntarily reported to the FBI, so they don't capture every case. The report also notes that AI chat generators can quickly write official-sounding emails that mimic a company's CEO or other officials, and that businesses reported more than $30 million in losses to BEC scams involving AI. It also says that not every BEC tactic is AI-enabled.

That's the point: AI makes the message more convincing, but the scam still depends on one moment, when someone changes a bank account without checking.

The warning signs

Europol lists the classic signals:

  • an unsolicited request, especially to change bank details;
  • pressure and a sense of urgency;
  • a request that skips your normal procedure;
  • a request for confidentiality, or unusual flattery.

The UK government's Stop! Think Fraud campaign adds one more that fits this season perfectly: requests to change bank details "especially near holidays or deadlines".

A callback routine that works

1. Never use the contact details in the request

Europol is explicit: don't use the phone number or email in the message asking for the change. Use the ones from earlier correspondence or your supplier records.

2. Call, and confirm both accounts

Ring the supplier on the known number. The UK guidance recommends confirming both the old and the new account details.

3. Add a second approver

No single person should be able to change a supplier's bank account. One person enters the change; another approves it after the callback.

4. Consider a waiting period

Stop! Think Fraud notes that some companies wait 14 or 30 days before new supplier bank details are approved, which gives time to check.

5. Confirm after paying

Europol suggests telling the supplier when you've paid, including the bank name and the last four digits of the account. If it's wrong, you find out fast.

6. Report attempts

Europol's advice: always contact the police about fraud attempts, even if you didn't lose money. If you did pay, call your bank immediately.

Brief your team this week

Before the year-end rush, send your finance and admin people one line: "We never change a supplier's bank details without a callback to a number we already have, and a second approval."
