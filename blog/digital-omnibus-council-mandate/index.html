The Council Has a Mandate, Not a New Law: What the Digital Omnibus Text Now Says on Cookies, Breaches and AI

A practical guide for privacy, security and product teams. This article is for general information only and is not legal advice.

On 21 October 2026, the Council of the European Union’s Permanent Representatives Committee (Coreper) agreed a negotiating mandate on the Digital Omnibus, the package that would amend the GDPR, the ePrivacy Directive and other digital rules. The official document, 14756/26, is dated 22 October 2026. It is the Council’s position for talks with the European Parliament. It is not a regulation, and it does not change the law that applies today.

That last point is the one to keep in view. A law firm analysis published on 9 November 2026 by Lewis Silkin confirms that the Parliament had not yet agreed its own position, so interinstitutional negotiations were not under way at that date. Lewis Silkin also reports that EU leaders aim to finish the simplification packages by the end of 2027. The final text can still change. Until it is adopted and applies, you still notify a personal data breach under the current Article 33, and you still apply today’s cookie and lawful-basis rules.

What the mandate does offer is a clearer official account of what national governments are prepared to take into the talks. Three practical points stand out: when a breach would have to be notified, how a “no” to cookies would have to work, and the reminder that building AI is not, by itself, a legitimate interest.

A mandate, not a rewrite you can implement tomorrow

Document 14756/26 is labelled “Mandate for negotiations with the European Parliament.” Delegations are told that the annex is the text agreed by Coreper on 21 October. Changes compared with the Commission proposal are marked in the document.

Earlier coverage of the Omnibus often had to work from the Commission’s proposal or from unofficial Council drafts. This article starts from the official mandate. It does not revisit the definition of personal data or the new pseudonymisation provisions, which are a separate debate, and it is not a piece about training models on your own users’ clicks.

If you want a wider walk-through of the package as a whole, The Digital Omnibus, Explained is the BookPilot guide to that landscape. Treat it as background reading, not as a substitute for the mandate or for the law now in force.

Breaches: a higher threshold, a longer clock, the same duty to write everything down

The Council text would recast Article 33(1) of the GDPR. Today, a controller must notify the supervisory authority of a personal data breach unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk to people’s rights and freedoms, and must do so without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than 72 hours after becoming aware of it.

In the mandate, notification to the authority would be required only “in the case of a personal data breach that is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.” The deadline would become “without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than 96 hours after having become aware of it.” If the notification is not made within 96 hours, it would have to be accompanied by reasons for the delay.

Two duties would not fall away. Recital 39 of the mandate says the higher threshold “does not affect the obligation of the controller to document the breach in accordance with paragraph 5 of Article 33,” or the accountability duty in Article 5(2). The same recital says the alignment of thresholds should not affect the duty to carry out an individual risk assessment. The Board would also be asked to publish lists of circumstances that are, and are not, likely to result in a high risk, and a common notification template. The absence of those lists would not excuse a failure to notify.

The entry point would be national, not a single EU-wide portal. Notification would be made “with the support of the national entry point” to be established under the NIS2 Directive. Until that national entry point exists, controllers would continue to notify the competent supervisory authority directly.

None of this is in force. If you have a breach this week, the current 72-hour rule and the current threshold still apply. Tools such as GDPRGard’s BreachNotify are built around those current Article 33 and 34 duties. Your Business Has Been Hacked is a practical guide for the hours after an incident. Do not retune your playbook to 96 hours or to “high risk only” on the strength of a mandate.

Cookies: a one-click “no”, and two different cooling-off periods

The mandate keeps the storage of, or access to, information on a user’s device inside a consent rule, with a list of strictly necessary purposes that would not need consent. Those purposes include carrying out a transmission, providing a service the user has expressly requested, certain forms of aggregated audience measurement, and maintaining or restoring security. Personalisation and commercial targeting are not on that exemption list.

Where consent is required, the user would have to be able to refuse “in an easy and intelligible manner with a single-click button or equivalent means.” If the user gives consent, there should be no new request for the same purpose for as long as that consent can lawfully be relied on. If the user refuses, a new request for the same purpose would be barred for a minimum cooling-off period.

That period is not the same in both instruments, and the two figures should not be merged. In the proposed new Article 88a of the GDPR, which would cover storing or accessing personal data in a natural person’s terminal equipment, the minimum period after a refusal is six months. In the proposed replacement of Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive, which covers storing or accessing information on a subscriber’s or user’s terminal equipment, the minimum period is four months. Both appear in the same mandate, in different legal acts.

The ePrivacy amendments would, under the mandate, have to be adopted and published by Member States, and applied, 24 months after adoption of the Omnibus regulation. Proposed Article 88a would apply from six months after the regulation enters into force. Those dates only start once there is a finished law.

Lewis Silkin’s 9 November note also records that the Commission’s idea of reducing cookie banners by centralising choices in the browser has been removed, and that cookie rules are expected to remain in the ePrivacy framework, with more situations where consent is not needed. That is consistent with the official text, but it is still only a negotiating position.

AI: legitimate interest is possible, “building AI” is not a basis by itself

Recital 33a of the mandate is the passage to quote when someone says the Omnibus will make training or running a model lawful automatically.

It says that processing personal data in the development or operation of an AI system or model “may be carried out for a legitimate interest of the controller… in accordance with Article 6(1)(f)… where appropriate,” unless those interests are overridden by the data subject’s interests or fundamental rights, in particular where the data subject is a child, or where other laws require consent. Public authorities are pointed towards Article 6(1)(e) where they develop or operate AI in the performance of a public task.

Then comes the sentence that does the real work: “It is important to clarify that the development and operation of an AI system or AI model does not in itself constitute a legitimate interest, and the applicability of Article 6(1)(f) to processing in the context of AI is to be assessed on a case-by-case basis, taking into account the underlying purpose of that processing.”

In other words, “we are building AI” is not a purpose. The purpose might be a recommendation engine, a fraud check, a support assistant or something else. That purpose still has to be identified, necessary and balanced. Appropriate organisational and technical measures would still be required, including data minimisation when sources are selected. Other GDPR provisions would remain unaffected.

What you should not change yet

Do not raise your internal breach-notification threshold to “high risk” on the basis of this text. Do not move your incident clock from 72 hours to 96 hours. Do not rebuild your consent interface around a six-month or four-month rule that does not yet exist, and do not pick one of those figures and ignore the other. Do not record “AI development” as a legitimate interest and leave it at that.

What you can usefully do now is more modest. Keep documenting every breach, including those you do not notify. Keep a clear record of why you notified or did not. Separate strictly necessary cookies from everything that needs consent, and make a refusal easy to give and effective. If you use personal data to develop or run a model, write down the actual purpose and the Article 6 basis that fits it.

Practical checklist: reading the Council mandate

  • ☐ Label the document correctly. It is a Council negotiating mandate (14756/26, 22 October 2026), agreed by Coreper on 21 October, not a new regulation.
  • ☐ Keep applying current law for breaches, cookies and lawful bases until a final text is adopted and applies.
  • ☐ Note the proposed breach changes: notification to the authority only where a high risk is likely; 96 hours where feasible; reasons if later; documentation under Article 33(5) remains; national entry point, with direct notification until it exists.
  • ☐ Do not drop your incident log. The mandate itself says documentation and an individual risk assessment would still be required.
  • ☐ Note the cookie changes: a single-click or equivalent refusal, and a cooling-off period after “no”.
  • ☐ Keep the two cooling-off periods distinct: six months in proposed Article 88a GDPR; four months in the proposed ePrivacy Article 5(3).
  • ☐ Remember the 24-month national transposition period foreseen for the ePrivacy amendments, and that Article 88a would apply from six months after entry into force — both only after adoption.
  • ☐ Read recital 33a in full. Legitimate interest may be used for AI where appropriate; developing or operating an AI system or model does not in itself constitute a legitimate interest.
  • ☐ Watch the Parliament’s position. As of 9 November 2026, Lewis Silkin reported that it had not yet been agreed, so trilogue talks had not started.
  • ☐ Do not redesign compliance around a text that can still change.

Conclusion

The Council now has an official mandate on the Digital Omnibus. That is progress, in the sense that we can finally quote a public text rather than a leak. It is not a reason to change how you notify a breach this month, or how your banner handles a “no”.

If this text survived unaltered, authorities would hear about fewer, more serious breaches, and they would have a little longer to be told; every breach would still have to be written down. Users would get a one-click refusal, with a pause before they are asked again — six months in one instrument, four in the other. And AI teams would still need a real purpose and a real Article 6 analysis, because “we are building AI” would not be a lawful basis on its own. Until the Parliament has spoken and a regulation is adopted, that is a map of the negotiation, not a change in your obligations.
