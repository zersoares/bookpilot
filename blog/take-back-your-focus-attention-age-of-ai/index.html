Your Attention Is the Product: Taking Back Your Focus Before the Year Ends

Sources consulted on 3 November 2026.

ZMBy Zirlandia Milkovic · 2026-11-07 · 4 min read

TL;DR

  • Gloria Mark's research found people spent about 150 seconds on one screen before switching in 2004. By 2016–2020 it was about 47 seconds.
  • About half of those switches are self-interruptions, not notifications.
  • Faster attention switching is linked to higher stress.
  • You don't need to quit technology. You need a few deliberate rules and some protected time.

If you've ever opened your phone to check one thing and surfaced twenty minutes later wondering where the time went, you're not alone, and it's not just a lack of willpower.

What the research says

Our attention on screens has shrunk

Gloria Mark, Chancellor's Professor of Informatics at the University of California, Irvine, has measured how long people stay on a single screen for about two decades. In 2004 her team found an average of about two and a half minutes. Around 2012 it was 75 seconds. Taking studies from 2016 to 2020 together, the average was 47 seconds, and the median was 40 seconds.

In her words, other researchers have replicated the result "within a few seconds".

We interrupt ourselves

It's tempting to blame the apps, and Mark agrees design plays a big part. But she also found that "half the time we interrupt ourselves": we go to check email or look something up without any external prompt.

Switching has a cost

Mark's research links faster attention switching with higher stress, measured with heart-rate monitors as well as questionnaires. She also points to the "switch cost": every time you change tasks, you need time to reorient.

Why it matters more with AI

AI tools can save us hours. They can also add one more tab, one more chat, one more stream of suggestions competing for the same limited attention. The goal isn't to use less AI. It's to decide where your attention goes, instead of letting every tool decide for you.

A weekend reset

You don't need a retreat. Try this over one weekend.

Saturday: notice

For one morning, keep a notepad next to you. Each time you pick up your phone or switch tabs, make a tick and, if you can, write why. You'll quickly see which switches were yours and which were triggered.

Saturday afternoon: remove the easy triggers

  • Turn off notifications for anything that isn't a person who needs you.
  • Move the apps you open on autopilot off your home screen.
  • Close the tabs you've been "saving for later" for weeks.

Sunday: plan protected time

Mark's research found people tend to have peaks of focused attention in the late morning and again in mid-afternoon. Look at your week and block two short focus sessions on your calendar, ideally in those windows, with your phone in another room.

Sunday evening: choose one purpose

Ask: what is one thing that matters to me this month that deserves my best attention? Write it down. Focus is easier when you know what it's for.

Make it stick

Start small. One protected session a day is better than an ambitious plan you drop by Wednesday. When you drift, notice it without judging yourself, and come back.
